<p>My guest today comes from a family of hairdressers and electricians, no doctors anywhere on the tree, and her own brother used to tell her she must have been swapped at birth. That joke was covering for something real: at 14, in a house gone chaotic with her brother's older friends and her parents elsewhere, she was touched in the night by someone in that circle, more than once, and never told a soul. Dr. Cindy Starke didn't call it trauma for another two decades. What she did instead is a masterclass in how childhood trauma hides in the body: she got out, got educated (an MD and a PhD in molecular genetics from Emory, after failing the SAT so many times she stopped counting), and built a 20-year career as a board-certified internal medicine physician without ever looking back.</p>

<p>Until her body made her look. Years of swallowing her own boundaries, in her marriage most of all, scarred her esophagus down from 15 millimeters to one or two: an actual, physical stricture, built one un-said "no" at a time. It took an empty COVID hospital and a year of forced stillness for her to remember what happened at 14 and finally process it. Now Dr. Starke runs "clearings," a trauma-release process she developed to help other women (nurses, mostly, at first) find their literal voice again, the same way she found hers.</p>

<p>We talk about the difference between a physician and a healer, the SAT she took seven times to win a scholarship her parents couldn't afford, and why she says the most uncomfortable thing you'll ever do is easier than living with an unhealed wound for the rest of your life.</p>
